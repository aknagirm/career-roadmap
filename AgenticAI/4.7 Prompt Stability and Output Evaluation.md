# 4.7 Prompt Stability and Output Evaluation

[← 4.6 LLM as a Judge Evaluation](<4.6 LLM as a Judge Evaluation.md>) · [↑ Chapter Overview](<4. Prompt Optimization and Evaluation.md>) · [Next Topic: 4.8 Evaluating Models: Cost vs. Performance Trade-offs →](<4.8 Evaluating Models - Cost vs Performance Tradeoffs.md>)

LLMs inherently produce variable outputs, even when the same input is repeated. This variation should be measured, understood, and managed at scale rather than assumed away.

Stability is relative to the workflow. Small wording changes may be harmless in creative or conversational tasks, while minor inconsistencies may be unacceptable in compliance, safety-critical, or decision-making systems.

## Prompt Stability

**Prompt stability** describes whether behavior across repeated runs is predictable, consistently correct, and acceptable for the task. Evaluation measures the distribution of outputs and determines whether its variation fits the application's reliability requirements.

Robust LLM systems quantify and manage variation instead of judging reliability from one output or trying to eliminate every difference.

### Types of Stability

- **Exact consistency** — Every run returns the same output. This is uncommon when temperature is above zero because sampling introduces randomness. Even at zero, some model APIs may not guarantee perfect determinism.
- **Quality consistency** — Wording or presentation may vary, but correctness, conclusions, and overall quality remain equivalent. This is the practical target for most workflows because it allows harmless surface variation.
- **Failure consistency** — The model fails in the same recognizable way across runs. Failure is undesirable, but a repeatable failure is easier to reproduce, diagnose, test, and fix than random errors.

These forms affect how reliably a system can be debugged, monitored, and improved. Workflows that depend on repeatable behavior should aim for at least quality consistency. A consistent failure should trigger refinement of the prompt, context, model, or broader workflow.

### Stability Spectrum

| Level | Behavior | Production suitability |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Quality, conclusions, and facts remain consistent across runs, even if phrasing changes slightly. | Required when the workflow is highly sensitive to variation. |
| **Medium** | Correctness and overall quality remain stable, but details or wording vary more noticeably. | Often acceptable when stylistic variation does not affect the outcome. |
| **Low** | The same prompt is sometimes correct and sometimes wrong on the same input. | Not production-ready because behavior is unpredictable. |

Production prompts should target high or medium stability according to the workflow's risk and tolerance for variation. Compliance, safety, and automated decision workflows generally require tighter stability than creative tasks.

Low stability creates incidents that are difficult to reproduce, debug, and trace to a root cause. Stability is therefore both an evaluation quality and a prerequisite for maintainable, reliable deployment.

## Measuring Stability

Use four complementary methods because each tests a different source of variation.

### 1. Repeated Trial Consistency

Run the same prompt and input multiple times. Report how often outputs reach the same answer using agreement percentage, majority share, quality variance, and failure rate.

High agreement indicates that the model aligns with itself, but does not prove that the agreed answer is correct. Compare the result with a trusted reference or rubric.

### 2. Sensitivity Analysis

Make small, meaning-preserving changes to the prompt, such as rewording instructions, reordering sections, or changing formatting and delimiters. Large changes in output indicate that the prompt is brittle and depends too heavily on presentation details.

### 3. Self-Consistency

For reasoning tasks, generate several independent solutions and compare their final answers. Agreement across different reasoning paths increases evidence of stability, while disagreement reveals uncertainty or brittleness.

### 4. Input Perturbation

Change superficial input features while preserving meaning, including casing, punctuation, whitespace, or synonyms. For example, a stable workflow should answer *“What is the capital of France?”*, *“what is the capital of france?”*, and *“capital of France”* consistently.

### Improving Brittle Prompts

When these tests reveal instability:

- Make instructions and expected outputs more explicit.
- Reduce dependence on formatting or surface wording.
- Add representative variations and edge cases to the evaluation set.
- Lower temperature when stochastic sampling is the main source of variation.

Lowering temperature can reduce sampling variation, but it will not fix unclear instructions or sensitivity to input wording.

## Self-Consistency and Majority Voting

**Self-consistency** runs the same task multiple times and aggregates the results. It reduces random single-run noise in workflows with a discrete decision or comparable final answer.

For example, a sentiment classifier might produce four results for one review:

```text
negative, negative, positive, negative
```

The majority decision is **negative** because three of four runs agree. An odd number of runs can reduce the chance of a tie. Free-form answers may need normalization or semantic grouping before they can be aggregated.

The idea resembles estimating the number of jelly beans in a jar: one estimate may be inaccurate, while combining many independent estimates often reduces random error. Use majority voting for categories and another suitable aggregation method, such as a median, for numerical results.

Self-consistency can support workflows such as:

- Content moderation
- Issue-tag routing
- Medical triage support
- Fraud flagging
- Legal-document review

It increases cost and latency because several model calls replace one. Run independent calls in parallel where appropriate, cap the number of samples, and monitor how often the aggregated result differs from a single-shot answer.

Use a fixed, recorded configuration when measuring self-consistency so results are comparable. A non-zero temperature can generate diverse reasoning paths; varying temperature should generally be treated as a separate sensitivity experiment.

Majority agreement is not proof of correctness because multiple runs may share the same systematic error. High-stakes use still requires task-specific validation and appropriate human oversight.

## Output Quality Dimensions

LLM output should be evaluated along four separate dimensions because strength in one does not guarantee strength in another.

### Accuracy or Correctness

Does the answer match ground truth or other verifiable facts? This dimension measures whether claims and conclusions are factually or logically correct.

### Relevance

Does the response address the user's underlying need rather than merely mention the same topic? An answer can be factually correct and on-topic while still being irrelevant or unhelpful for the requested task.

### Faithfulness

Are the claims supported by the resources or context provided to the model? Faithfulness is the main dimension for evaluating hallucination and source grounding.

Accuracy and faithfulness are different. An answer can state an externally true fact that does not appear in the supplied source, making it accurate but unfaithful to that source.

### Confidence

Do the model's stated or apparent confidence signals align with observed correctness? Fluent, assertive language does not prove accuracy because models can sound equally confident when right or wrong.

### Report Dimensions Separately

These dimensions can diverge. A response may be relevant but incomplete, accurate but unsupported by the given source, or confident but incorrect. Report all four scores separately to support precise diagnosis and improvement. If an aggregate score is also required, retain the individual component scores so important weaknesses are not hidden.

## Hallucination Categories

Hallucination is a signature LLM failure in which unsupported or incorrect content is presented fluently and confidently. Unlike an explicit error, this output may initially appear trustworthy. Traditional software can also produce silent errors, but LLMs make plausible fabrication a recurring evaluation concern.

| Type | Description | Example | Detection focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intrinsic hallucination** | Directly contradicts the supplied source. | The source says `2023`, but the response says `2024`. | Compare claims directly with source facts. |
| **Extrinsic hallucination** | Adds information that the source never states or supports. | The response says the document contains claim X, but X is absent. | Match each claim to supporting evidence. |
| **Compositional hallucination** | Combines individually true facts into a false conclusion. | Correct premises are connected through an invalid inference. | Validate reasoning and relationships, not only individual facts. |
| **Attribution error** | Connects a correct fact to the wrong source or citation. | An accurate statement cites a passage that does not support it. | Verify each citation against its exact claim. |
| **Continuity error** | Creates an incorrect timeline, sequence, or narrative relationship. | Events are described in an order that conflicts with the source. | Check temporal order and cross-section consistency. |

These categories require different detection and mitigation strategies. Intrinsic contradictions may be caught through direct source comparison, while compositional and continuity errors often require deeper reasoning or relationship checks.

## Hallucination Detection

Detect hallucinations by comparing individual output claims with their supporting sources.

### Citation Verification

Check that every cited passage exists and actually supports the associated claim. A valid-looking citation that points to an unrelated passage is still an attribution error. Missing sources may indicate extrinsic hallucination.

### Claim Extraction and Source Matching

Break the response into atomic, verifiable claims. Retrieve candidate evidence for each claim and classify it as:

- Supported by the source
- Contradicted by the source
- Unsupported because the source contains insufficient evidence

This provides more precise evidence than scoring the response only as a whole.

### LLM Judge with a Faithfulness Rubric

A calibrated LLM judge can assess each claim against the supplied source using explicit faithfulness criteria. The judge can scale review, but its decisions should be validated against human-labeled examples and should not be treated as ground truth.

### Embedding Similarity

Compare claim embeddings with candidate source passages to identify possible supporting evidence. Low similarity can flag a claim for review, but high similarity does not prove support because embeddings may miss negation, numerical differences, or subtle contradictions.

### Schema Validation

For structured output, validate required fields, data types, allowed values, and unexpected fields. Schema validation catches structural problems and misplaced content, but valid structure does not prove factual accuracy.

Hallucination cannot be guaranteed to reach zero. The practical goal is to detect unsupported claims, measure their rate, define acceptable limits for the task, and monitor changes over time.

### Faithfulness Score

A simple claim-level faithfulness score is:

```text
faithfulness = supported claims / total verifiable claims
```

This requires consistent rules for extracting claims and deciding what counts as sufficient source support. Track the score over representative examples and report unsupported or contradicted claims separately when their risks differ.

## Confidence Does Not Equal Correctness

Fluent, authoritative language can accompany an incorrect answer. Confidence signals must therefore be measured and calibrated rather than inferred from tone.

### Log Probabilities

Log probabilities represent how likely the model considered the generated tokens under its probability distribution. They can help with short, discrete outputs such as classification labels or concise Q&A.

For long-form generation, token-level probabilities are harder to interpret because many dependent tokens contribute to the answer. A high-probability sentence can still contain a factually incorrect claim.

### Stated Confidence

The model can be asked to report its confidence, but this self-assessment is often overconfident or poorly calibrated. It may be used as one supporting signal after validation, but never as ground truth.

### Self-Consistency

Run the same task multiple times and measure agreement among final answers. If the model returns the same answer in nine of ten independent runs, that agreement is more informative than one confident response. If it gives nine different answers with equally confident wording, the workflow is unstable.

Runs should be independent: use fresh requests with the same recorded configuration and do not include previous generated answers in later contexts. Otherwise, later responses may be influenced by earlier ones.

Self-consistency still does not prove correctness because every run may repeat the same systematic error. Compare agreement with trusted answers, source evidence, or task-specific checks.

### Practical Guidance

Calibrate each confidence signal against observed correctness. Evaluate whether higher log probability, stated confidence, or run agreement actually corresponds to better accuracy on a representative dataset.

Use patterns of independently measured behavior rather than confident wording, while retaining direct correctness and faithfulness checks.
