# 4.1.1 Model Parameters

[← 4.1 Inputs to Large Language Models](<4.1 Inputs to Large Language Models.md>) · [↑ Chapter Overview](<4. Prompt Optimization and Evaluation.md>) · [Next Topic: 4.2 Need for Prompt Evaluation →](<4.2 Need for Prompt Evaluation.md>)

Model parameters shape how a model expresses its response rather than changing its underlying capabilities.

## Max Tokens

**Max tokens** sets a hard upper limit on the generated output. Generation stops when the limit is reached, even if the response is incomplete. It does not require the model to produce exactly that many tokens; it only allows the model to generate up to that amount.

This parameter affects:

- **Cost** — Longer outputs consume more generated tokens.
- **Latency** — Longer responses generally take more time to generate.
- **Format reliability** — JSON, tables, rubrics, and multi-part answers need enough room to finish correctly.

If the limit is too low, the output may end mid-sentence or mid-structure. For example, truncated JSON can break a downstream parser. When debugging an incomplete answer, check whether the model failed at the task or simply ran out of available tokens.

Short labels and classification tasks can use low limits. Summaries, transcripts, detailed explanations, and complex structured outputs usually need higher limits.

## Temperature

**Temperature** controls randomness by reshaping the probability distribution of possible next tokens. It determines how strongly the model favors the most likely candidates.

- **Low temperature** produces more focused and predictable responses. It is suitable for factual Q&A, classification, code generation, evaluations, and structured outputs where consistency matters.
- **High temperature** gives lower-probability tokens a greater chance of selection. It is useful for brainstorming, creative writing, naming, ideation, and generating diverse alternatives.

For example, a low-temperature cybersecurity tagline might be: *“Protecting your data, securing your future.”* A higher temperature might produce: *“Your digital fortress in an unpredictable world.”*

Neither setting is always better. Low temperature improves consistency but can produce repetitive or generic answers. High temperature increases creativity but also raises the risk of hallucinations, formatting errors, and off-task responses. Tasks with one correct answer generally favor lower temperatures, while tasks with many acceptable answers can benefit from higher temperatures.

## Top-p (Nucleus Sampling)

**Top-p** controls the candidate pool using cumulative probability rather than a fixed token count. It selects the smallest group of likely next tokens whose combined probability reaches the chosen threshold.

For example, with `Top-p = 0.9`, the model includes likely tokens until their cumulative probability reaches 90%. The pool adapts to the context:

- When the model is confident, a few tokens may cover most of the probability.
- When the model is uncertain, more tokens may be needed to reach the threshold.

This differs from **Top-k**, which always considers a fixed number of tokens. A low Top-p narrows the pool and makes responses more conservative and predictable. A high Top-p widens the pool and allows more varied or unexpected wording.

Top-p also interacts with temperature. High temperature encourages randomness, while low Top-p restricts the available choices. Using both aggressively can produce conflicting effects and make behavior harder to tune.

A practical approach is to start by tuning temperature. Adjust Top-p only when finer control over diversity is needed, and avoid changing temperature, Top-p, and Top-k at the same time.

## Top-k

**Top-k** limits token selection to the `K` most likely candidates at each generation step. For example, with `Top-k = 40`, the model can choose only from the 40 most probable next tokens, excluding every other option.

- **Low Top-k** provides fewer choices, making output more focused and predictable.
- **High Top-k** expands the candidate pool, allowing greater variety.

A low Top-k works well for predictable factual text. In a sentence such as *“The capital of France is...”*, the most likely candidates will include *“Paris.”* Creative writing may need a larger pool because a low Top-k can exclude unusual but suitable options, producing bland or repetitive text.

Unlike Top-p, Top-k does not adapt to the model's confidence. It applies the same fixed limit whether the context has only a few strong choices or many plausible ones. This is why Top-p is often preferred for adaptive control.

Top-k is useful when a strict cap on randomness is required. In most prompt-evaluation workflows, keep it stable and tune temperature or Top-p first.

## Frequency Penalty

**Frequency penalty** reduces the likelihood of reusing a token based on how often it has already appeared in the generated text. The more frequently the token appears, the stronger the penalty becomes.

For example, a model might produce: *“This product is reliable, reliable, and reliable for reliable teams.”* A frequency penalty lowers the chance of repeatedly selecting *“reliable”* and encourages more varied wording.

It is useful for:

- Long-form content
- Brainstorming and open-ended writing
- Marketing copy
- Outputs with unnecessary word or phrase repetition

Use it carefully because repetition is sometimes required for correctness and structure. JSON, SQL, source code, legal language, medical terminology, and technical documentation may need repeated field names, keywords, or precise terms. Penalizing them can damage the format or cause the model to replace an accurate term with an awkward or less precise synonym.

Use frequency penalty when repetition harms readability. Avoid or minimize it when repetition is necessary for structure, precision, or consistency.

## Presence Penalty

**Presence penalty** reduces the likelihood of reusing a token or idea once it has appeared, regardless of how many times it was used. In practice, this nudges the model away from previously covered wording and concepts so it explores new directions.

For example, when brainstorming product features, a presence penalty can discourage the model from producing many variations of one theme and encourage ideas from different categories.

The two repetition penalties have different purposes:

- **Frequency penalty** increases with repeated use and mainly helps reduce repeated wording.
- **Presence penalty** applies after the first use and mainly encourages new concepts and broader coverage.

Use frequency penalty when the model repeats the same phrase. Use presence penalty when it keeps circling the same idea using different words.

Presence penalty is most useful for brainstorming and ideation. It is less useful for factual extraction, classification, and code generation, where repeated terms or concepts may be required. High values can make responses strange or imprecise by discouraging necessary language.

Use presence penalty when novelty and broader coverage matter. Avoid or minimize it when the task requires precision and consistent terminology.

## Stop Sequences

**Stop sequences** instruct the model to halt when specified text appears. They stop generation based on content, while max tokens stops it after a length limit is reached.

Examples include:

- Stopping at a delimiter that marks the end of a section
- Using a marker such as `\nUser:` to prevent a chat model from generating the next user message
- Ending after a designated marker for a JSON block or other structured output
- Preventing extra explanations after the required answer

A period could be used to request a single sentence, but this is risky because periods also appear in abbreviations and may stop the response too early.

Stop sequences are valuable when model output feeds into parsers, business logic, or other downstream systems. Unwanted commentary or continuation can otherwise break the expected format.

Choose sequences carefully. A common sequence may stop generation prematurely, while a rare sequence may never appear. A useful approach is to combine both controls: use max tokens to prevent runaway length and a stop sequence to end generation at the intended boundary.

Because model parameters interact, change one parameter at a time and version the configuration alongside the prompt.
